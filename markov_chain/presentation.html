Bebop as a Markov Chain

A discrete-time finite-state random walk on a graph of 428 states, estimated from Charlie Parker's improvisational lines.

Research question. Can the syntactic rules of bebop improvisation (voice leading, harmonic gravity, the on-beat / off-beat asymmetry) be recovered as numerical properties of a Markov-chain operator estimated from Parker's solos?

2 · State space

State = (scale degree, chord quality, beat position)

Three discrete coordinates per event (note or rest):

  • Scale degree d ∈ {0, …, 11} ∪ {R}: for a note, semitones above the active chord root d = (pcnote − pcchord-root) mod 12; for a rest, the sentinel R.
  • Chord quality q ∈ Q = {maj7, min7, dom7, m7♭5, dim7}: the active chord at event onset (rests inherit it).
  • Beat position b ∈ {b1, b2, b3, b4, &1, &2, &3, &4}: all 50 tunes are 4/4, so an event either lands on one of the four beats or falls offbeat, labelled by the beat it follows.

Why this state space

  • Scale degree abstracts over transpositions: the same line over F7 and over B♭7 collapses to one state.
  • Chord quality respects the functional context: a "♭7" sounds like the dominant 7th, the half-diminished 7th, or the dim7 depending on quality.
  • Beat position separates structural notes (downbeats) from passing material (see bebop's passing notes).

3 · The encoding, on a real solo

Anthropology, bar by bar

Result

|S| ≤ 13 × 5 × 8 = 520; observed: 428. The encoding collapses 23,143 events onto those 428 states: about 54 visits per state, and every tune revisits most of its states several times over. That recurrence is what makes the transition probabilities estimable at all.

Important: we are not considering durations, octaves (heights), dynamics (f, p, pp) etc.

4 · The other 92

What Parker never plays

428 of 520 states occur. The 92 that never do split into two different causes.

Scarcity, 90 cells. Each quality has its own 13 × 8 = 104-cell grid. dom7 fills all 104, but m7♭5 and dim7 carry only ~165 events each and cannot cover theirs.

Some of the other states are simply never used because they contain a note that clashes with the chord, such as a major third over a minor chord.

5 · The operator, concretely

P is a table: 428 rows, one question each

Every row asks one question: Parker is on this state, where does he go next? Walk the 23,143 events in order; each time state i is followed by state j, add 1 to cell (i, j), then divide each row by its own total. The hat marks an estimate: P is what 50 solos say about the unobservable true P. Probabilities live within a row: each row sums to 1 across all 428 columns. Much of the emptiness is metre: from &4 the walk goes to b1 69 % of the time or stays in &4 21 %, and reaches b3 just 0.4 % of the time. Most cells are empty because the beat cannot get there. Reading the colour: dark dots are moves Parker actually plays; near-white means the model is confident a move does not happen; pink means it has almost no data for that row and is spreading the probability evenly over all 428 states.

6 · The assumption

The Markov property: is the current state enough?

Pr(Xt+1 | X0, …, Xt = si)  =  Pr(Xt+1 | Xt = si)

The next state depends only on where the line is, not on how it got there. Everything before the current event is discarded.

This is the licence for the previous slide. If history mattered, Parker could not be summarised as a table of "from → to".

Take 7 (5), dom7, &4, which occurs 346 times with a known predecessor, and split its continuations by what came before:

arrived frommost likely continuation
9 (6), dom7, b4  n = 630 (1), dom7, b1   22 %
4 (3), dom7, b4  n = 4510 (b7), dom7, &4   40 %

Same current state, different futures. Approached from the 3rd (semitone 4), the line keeps descending to the ♭7 (semitone 10) four times out of ten; approached from the 6th, that continuation barely appears. The property is false in the strict sense, and we know the direction of the error: descending lines carry momentum a first-order chain cannot see.

9 · Stationary distribution

Where the walk spends its time

Let the walk run a long time and count how often it is in each state. That settles to a fixed share per state, written π: one number per state, adding to 1.

π P = π

π is the distribution that one more step leaves unchanged.

Why that matters. π comes out of the transitions, never from how common each state is, yet it reproduces the corpus's own frequencies. A check on the whole estimation.

The three busiest states

  1. 7 (5), dom7, &4    π = 0.0141
  2. 0 (1), dom7, &4    π = 0.0137
  3. 7 (5), dom7, &3    π = 0.0128

One dot per state: π against how often that state actually occurs. The diagonal is perfect agreement.

12 · PageRank

Random surfer with damping

PageRank runs a walk with one change: 85 % of the time follow an edge, otherwise jump to a state chosen at random. The teleport keeps the walk from being trapped anywhere, so the ranking is unique whatever the graph looks like. Alongside it, betweenness asks a different question of the same graph: not where the walk spends time, but which states sit on the likely routes between other states. 428 nodes, 5,421 directed edges.

Where the walk spends its time. Nine of the ten are dominant sevenths, eight offbeat.

Which states the routes run through. Eight of the ten are rests, nine on the beat.

Offbeat notes are where the line lives; rests on the beat are where it passes through.

14 · Where it resolves to

Not just how long resolution takes, but onto which note

Slide 13 asked how many events pass before the line lands in the resolved set. The same linear system answers a second question at no extra cost: which member of that set does it reach first?

B = (I − Q)−1 R

Row i is a distribution over the 73 resolved states: starting unresolved at i, where does the line first come to rest. Rows sum to 1.

Four of the six resolve by a semitone onto the nearest chord tone, between 49 % and 66 % of the time. A fifth, the 2nd over a major chord, falls a whole tone to the root. Nothing told the model what a semitone is.

The bottom row is slide 3's move, the minor third over a major chord going to the major third, 61 %. The pitch is voice leading; the timing is metre, every target landing on the next strong beat.

15 · Voice-leading recovery

The V7→I half-step resolution as a single matrix cell

Joint transition probability under the stationary distribution:

Pr(Xt = i, Xt+1 = j)  =  πi · Pij

Ranking those cells shows which transitions actually carry the traffic.

The canonical V7 → I cell

Theory says the ♭7 of a dominant falls a half-step to the major 3rd of the tonic. In this state space that is one cell: (♭7, dom7, &4) → (3, maj7, ♩1).

16 · Open questions

Questions I didn't have time to address

  • How long until Parker plays the same thing again? How often each state comes back round.
  • What does a solo look like backwards? Whether the language still makes sense played in reverse, and by how much it does not.
  • What does tension look like when it never resolves? The shape of a line that keeps postponing the landing.
  • How surprising is the next note, on average? One number for how predictable the whole language is.
  • Does the vocabulary split into neighbourhoods? Whether the line settles inside a region for a while before moving on.
  • Is resolution reliable or erratic? The spread of the waiting times, not only their average.
  • How long before the walk has played everything it knows?

17 · Take-aways

What the operator did and did not recover

What it recovered

  • Voice leading, as one cell. From the ♭7 of a dominant in the pickup, the next note is the major 3rd of the tonic on the downbeat 10.71 % of the time. The neighbouring cells sit under 0.1 %: the rule is specific to one source position and one target beat.
  • Gravity, as a waiting time. A typical state reaches a chord tone on a beat in 4.1 events; the most unstable states get there in under 2. The notes that resolve fastest are the ones theory calls approach tones, and the chain found them without being told which notes clash.
  • The on-beat / offbeat asymmetry. All eight fastest-resolving states sit offbeat. Tension is placed off the beat and released on it.

What it did not

  • Markov might not be the best tool. Approached from the 3rd, that same state behaves differently than approached from the 6th.
  • Much of the structure is metre, not music. The operator is 97 % empty largely because the beat cannot jump: from &4 the walk goes to b1 69 % of the time. Some of what looks like syntax is bar lines.
  • The sampled lines are not Parker. Same interval sizes, same rest frequency, no phrases. The engraved staff is the honest version of that: it holds up bar to bar and falls apart across a chorus.
  • Other dimensions are missing. No register, no duration, no dynamics. The state is a pitch class against a chord, on a beat, and nothing else.

18 · Further work

What I would do next

Splitting the operator in two

One operator was estimated over all transitions. Splitting it and comparing the halves is the obvious extension, and a split earns its place only if it conditions on something not already in the state and not explained by metre.

  • Within-chord versus across-chord. Does the line behave differently at a chord change than inside one? The flag exists in the data and was never used.
  • Direction (9,505 up, 11,530 down) and step versus leap (11,226 / 8,441). Both are properties of the transition rather than the source, and both are well balanced. Descending lines already land a chord tone on a strong beat 67 % of the time against 54 % ascending, z = 12.4, so there is something there.
  • Phrase position: first note after a rest versus mid-phrase.

Other threads

  • More coordinates: duration, register, dynamics. None are modelled. Duration and register are already in the data, absolute pitch is stored and only the degree is used. Dynamics are not: these transcriptions carry no dynamic marks at all, so that coordinate needs a performance source rather than the score.
  • Interval vectors. Walk on the interval content of a short window of the line instead of on one note against one chord. It throws away which note is which and keeps only the distances, so it is a coarser description, but it is the same for a phrase and its transposition, which the current encoding is not.

Extra · Evolving the distribution

Put all the probability on one state and let it run

Section 2 found π algebraically, as the left eigenvector at λ = 1. Here it is found the other way, by iteration:

μ0 = ei ,    μt+1 = μt P

Start with certainty about the note, and watch the certainty dissolve.

Starting on the busiest state, (5, dom7, &4):

  • after 2 steps the total-variation distance to π is already below 0.5;
  • after 21 steps it is below 0.01;
  • at t = 40 the largest disagreement with π is 8.7 × 10−6.

The decay tracks |λ2|t almost exactly, which is the mixing time of Section 3 measured rather than asserted.

Extra · Spectral analysis

The spectrum contains a metric cycle

Every step the walk forgets a little of where it started, and it forgets at a fixed rate: whatever you still know shrinks to 81 % of itself each step. After five steps about a third is left, after twenty almost nothing.

τmix ≈ 1 / (1 − |λ2|)

λ20.596 + 0.555 i
|λ2|0.8143
τmix5.4 steps

λ2 is complex, and that is the result

A plain number would mean the walk just fades toward π. A complex one means it also circles, returning to similar territory at a regular interval:

2π / arg(λ2) = 2π / 0.750 = 8.4 events

One bar of eighth notes is exactly 8 events. The metre is in the operator, and nothing put it there: it was built by tallying which state follows which.

One dot per eigenvalue. The blob near the centre is short-lived patterns; the star at 1.0 is π, which never decays; the star off the axis is the circling mode, and its angle is the 8.4-event period.

Extra · Coming back round

How long until Parker plays this state again

Hitting time looks forward to a set the walk has not reached yet. Recurrence time asks the opposite: standing on a state now, how many events pass before the line plays it again.

mi = 1 / πi

Kac's formula, so π is the whole answer. No new estimation.

The busiest state, the fifth of a dominant on the pickup, returns every 71 events, roughly nine bars of eighth notes. The median state waits 809.

So Parker's core vocabulary comes round about once a chorus, and the rest of the state space is genuinely occasional.

Read the right-hand spike with care. Those are states with almost no data, whose π is mostly smoothing. Their recurrence times say more about α than about Parker.
